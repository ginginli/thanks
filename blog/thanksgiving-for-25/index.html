How a Midwestern Mom Cooks Thanksgiving for 25

Real-world lessons from a veteran host — the part portion calculators can't teach you

Every November, kitchen-table math gets done all over the country: How many pounds? How many pies? Will this be enough? Portion calculators — including ours — will happily answer those questions. But when we spoke with hosts who have actually fed a house of twenty-five, a different truth kept coming up: the calculator gets you started; knowing your family gets you finished.

The scene: 25 people, give or take

One host we heard from has run Thanksgiving for a rotating crowd of 23–27 for years. The "give or take" isn't sloppy counting — it's a Midwestern institution: plates packed for relatives who couldn't make it. Somebody's grandmother is laid up; somebody's son is on shift. The food still travels to them. Any accurate headcount is therefore a floor, not a ceiling.

The menu, and the strategy behind it

Her approach isn't per-person precision — it's maximum flexibility: the largest turkey the store had, plus a whole baked ham as a second anchor. Around those two anchors: a five-pound bag of potatoes for mashing, a 9×16 pan of mac and cheese, a huge broccoli casserole, four boxes of stuffing dressed up and baked, green bean casserole, baked beans, frozen corn, pickles, olives, cranberry sauce, giblet gravy, and a Midwestern fruit salad known in her family only as "pink stuff."

And close to fifty homemade dinner rolls — because in her words, the rolls and the broccoli casserole are what people fight over and take home. The pie situation was handled with similar pragmatism: guests bring desserts, but she bakes one pumpkin pie anyway, "just in case nobody brings one."

Four lessons worth stealing

1. Buffer for the people who aren't in the room

To-go plates and absent relatives mean the effective headcount is always higher than the guest list. If you're new to hosting, take whatever the calculator says and add five people — that's the field-tested rule of thumb for covering big eaters and packed plates. Our planner's Heavy +50% leftover mode exists for exactly this scenario.

2. The sides get fought over more than the turkey

Everybody remembers the turkey, but the rolls, the potatoes, and the one casserole your family loves are what vanish first. If you plan leftovers, plan them where the demand actually is. In our planner, the Some +30% mode buffers the turkey along with the take-home favorites — potatoes and rolls.

3. Full batches beat half batches

It's hard to predict which side will be popular on any given year, and the price difference between a full pan and a half pan is usually a few dollars. Veteran hosts don't bother halving recipes — they cook the full batch and let the leftovers sort themselves out. Precision is for turkeys; generosity is for sides.

4. The potluck endgame

The final twist in her story: these days, the family switched to a potluck with assigned dishes, and hosting became dramatically less stressful. It's a different, smaller family now — but she still makes the homemade rolls and "that darned good broccoli casserole" every single year, because some things aren't negotiable.

Start with the math, finish with the family

Use a calculator to get in the neighborhood — then adjust with what you know: which uncle takes seconds of stuffing, which cousin's new partner is vegetarian, and whose container gets filled before they leave. That knowledge is the best portion calculator there is.
